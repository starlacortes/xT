Three directions, three lineages

The §7 identity is locked — wordmark, ◉ mark, palette, type, discretion. What follows grows that system into the product surfaces. “Rooted in history” is taken literally: each direction extends the identity from a real lineage behind the name — the first isoline charts (1701–1768), the founding mathematics of pharmacokinetics (1910–1937), and the isoclinic instrument tradition. Every mockup renders the same protocol through the same Bateman superposition the app runs, so what differs is direction, never data.

The constant

One protocol, rendered by every direction below. This is the dataset the mockups share — computed live by the page, not drawn by hand:

Estradiol cypionate · 5 mg IM · every 7 days · one lab draw

  • Anchored. One blood draw at day 10 reads 180 pg/mL; the curve is solid up to the draw.
  • Modeled. Beyond the last observation the curve goes dashed — the brand’s honesty device, made literal in every direction.
  • Today. Day 24, the rose marker on the dashed projection: wherever the math puts it, not a chosen number.
  • Type, palette, mark, discretion. Plex Serif / Mono / Hyperlegible, rose + lavender OKLCH, the ◉ bullseye — unchanged across all three directions.
I

Halleyan Lines

Halley 1701 · Whiston 1721 · Wilcke 1768

Halley’s 1701 Atlantic chart drew “curved lines” through points of equal magnetic variation — the first isolines ever engraved, printed, and published. Navigators called them Halleyan lines for a century. Where the compass stood true, he drew a double line. Whiston charted the first inclination lines for southern England (1719–21); Wilcke’s 1768 Neigungscharte was the first near-global isoclinic chart — equal dip, the word’s first home. This direction keeps the locked identity and extends it with the first charts’ grammar: hairline weights, the value written along the line, and the double rule for steady.

Value along the line. Halley numbered his lines with the degrees they carried; the plateau carries its own value.
Double rule for steady. Where the curve enters its plateau, a second hairline — the “compass stands true” mark.
Engraved slope field. The brand hero’s dashes, dialed to etching weight.
Cartouche readout. The level sits in a double-ruled frame, chart-margin style.
Refuses: parchment stains, compass roses, sepia clichés, “antique” ornament.
Refuses: any texture that fights the hairlines at small sizes.

II

Chart Paper

Bateman 1910 · Teorell 1937

The app’s engine is superposed Bateman functions. Bateman solved that exact system for Rutherford’s radioactive chains in 1910 (Proc. Cambridge Phil. Soc. 15:423–427). Twenty-seven years later, Teorell’s two papers — Kinetics of distribution of substances administered to the body (Arch. Int. Pharmacodyn. Thér. 57:205–240) — founded pharmacokinetics as a quantitative science. That founding era worked on graph paper: ruled grids, instrument traces, values in a lettering mono. The brand’s instrument-panel voice (Plex Mono itself descends from the Selectric era) grows here into the whole surface — and the honesty device becomes the signature: solid where the model is anchored, dashed where it projects.

Chart-paper ground. Fine 1 mm rules with major lines, in brand line colors — never behind prose.
Anchored / modeled split. The last lab draw is the seam; the projection carries the dash.
Lab annotations. Leader lines and mono callouts, the way a lab notebook records a sample.
Log-line readout. The level reads like an entry: value, unit, state, day.
Refuses: skeuomorphic paper, tape, coffee rings, any grunge.
Refuses: dashboard-generic grids. The grid serves the trace or it goes.

III

The Settling Arc

Whiston 1721 · Wilcke 1768 · the dip needle

An isoclinic chart maps where a needle settles: lines of equal inclination. The instrument is the dip circle — a needle that swings, loses energy, and comes to rest on its line. Whiston’s straight-line inclination charts (1719–21) and Wilcke’s 1768 world chart drew the equilibrium of a moving system — exactly what a zero-growth isocline means in the ODE sense the app’s name borrows. This direction turns the level readout into that instrument: a graduated arc beneath the curve, the needle dipping to today’s reading, its recent swings drawn as fading ghosts — “you are here” is where the needle stops.

The dip circle. A horizontal zero and graduated arc beneath today’s point; the rose needle dips to the level.
Oscillation ghosts. The needle’s last few swings, fading — motion that means “finding equilibrium.”
Strict scope. An arc only ever means “a level reading.” Never nav, never cards, never decoration.
“Find your line.” The brief’s shortest tagline, which this direction earns literally.
Refuses: gauges, dials-for-drama, brass, skeuomorphic compasses.
Refuses: letting a second motif family compete with the Bateman curve.

Method & sources

Every curve on this page is computed in-page by the same superposition the app runs: one Bateman term per dose (ka 1.2 d⁻¹; ke = ln2/8.5 d⁻¹ for cypionate’s 8.5-day half-life), scaled so the day-10 draw reads 180 pg/mL. Today’s value is whatever the math says. Halley’s 1701 chart and the “Halleyan lines” / double-line history: Royal Society presentation, June 1701; the term “isogonic” came a century later (Hansteen, c. 1820). Whiston’s inclination charts: 1719–1721, southern England. Wilcke’s Versuch einer magnetischen Neigungscharte: 1768, first near-global isoclinic chart. Bateman: The solution of a system of differential equations occurring in the theory of radioactive transformations, read 21 Feb 1910. Teorell: two 1937 papers, Archives internationales de pharmacodynamie et de thérapie 57:205–240 — the founding of pharmacokinetics. Type: IBM Plex (Abbink / Bold Monday; Plex Serif from Bodoni + Janson, Mono from the Selectric) — the locked identity is already an artifact of design history.
